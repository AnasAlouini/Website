Beginners Guide for Running

Whether you're lacing up for the first time or getting back on track — this is where you start.

Whether you're lacing up your first pair of running shoes or want to start running again after a long break, heading out for a run may not feel easy right now. The good news is that starting your running journey can be simple. You just need to know where to start, how to train, and why it matters to you.

Benefits of Running

Every runner has their own reason for getting into running, whether it's for social fun or fitness. Research shows this activity offers plenty of physical and mental health benefits, including:

  • Improved mental health: Sustained exercise encourages your body to release endorphins — our natural painkillers and mood elevators. Running may help decrease symptoms of depression, stress, and anxiety.
  • Stronger cardiovascular system: Consistent running may help strengthen the heart and improve circulation, leading to a lower resting heart rate and blood pressure.
  • Healthier bones, muscles, and joints: The weight-bearing impact of moderate running with proper form and footwear may improve bone density, making your bones stronger.

Types of Running

  • Recovery Run: A very short, slow-paced run done 1–2 days after a tough workout to boost blood flow and ease muscle soreness. Should feel very comfortable and conversational.
  • Easy Run: A comfortable, relaxed pace that builds cardiovascular endurance. Should feel like a 2–3 out of 10 in intensity — you can speak in full sentences.
  • Long Run: A weekly, low-intensity run lasting 60–90 minutes at a conversational pace. It's the slowest, longest run of the week.
  • Fartlek: Swedish for "speed play" — a flexible method that mixes fast running with slow recovery jogs without stopping. Improves speed and stamina.
  • Tempo Run: A "comfortably hard" sustained run designed to raise the lactate threshold, allowing you to run faster for longer.
